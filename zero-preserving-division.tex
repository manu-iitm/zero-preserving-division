\begin{abstract}
This paper proposes a division calculus in which division by zero is not
interpreted as undefined, but neither is it treated as ordinary division by
an invertible element. A distinguished algebraic element $I$ is introduced
through the division-calculus resolution of $1/0$, while multiplication
remains zero-preserving: $0x=x0=0$. The element $I$ is idempotent,
$I^2=I$, and division by $I$ resolves to multiplication by zero.

The central feature of the proposal is that division is treated as a
syntax-sensitive computational operation rather than as an unrestricted
binary operation on algebraic values. In particular, denominators containing
$0$ or $I$ are resolved by explicit singular-factor rules before ordinary
algebraic simplification is permitted. Chained division is right-associated
and evaluated from the innermost division outward. Singular factors generated
by an inner division must be retained when that division expression occurs
inside an outer denominator.

Consequently, familiar algebraic transformations such as cancellation,
arbitrary denominator simplification, and unrestricted reassociation of
division are not valid in the singular cases. The resulting framework
preserves ordinary arithmetic away from $0$ and $I$ while providing a
consistent operational treatment of expressions involving these two
distinguished elements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Division by zero is traditionally excluded from ordinary arithmetic.
This exclusion is natural within fields because division is defined through
multiplicative inverses, and zero has no multiplicative inverse.

This paper explores a different approach.

Rather than declaring every expression involving division by zero to be
undefined, we introduce a distinguished element $I$ associated with the
division expression
\[
\frac{1}{0}.
\]

The crucial point is that this association does \emph{not} turn $0$ into an
ordinary invertible element. Instead, the notation $1/0$ is a special
division expression that resolves to a separate algebraic value $I$.

The framework therefore separates two concepts:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item an underlying algebra of values containing $0$, $1$, and $I$;
    \item a division calculus that specifies how division expressions are
    resolved.
\end{enumerate}

This distinction is essential. Ordinary algebraic identities continue to
apply to resolved values, but they cannot be used indiscriminately to
rewrite an unresolved singular denominator.

For example, the framework intentionally permits
\[
\frac{1}{0}=I
\]
while retaining
\[
I0=0.
\]

Thus $I$ is not the ordinary multiplicative inverse of zero.

Similarly,
\[
\frac{I}{I}=0
\]
rather than $1$.

The purpose of this paper is to define these rules precisely, demonstrate
their consequences, and identify the algebraic transformations that must be
restricted in the presence of singular denominators.

\section{Underlying Algebra}

\subsection{The distinguished element}

We introduce a distinguished element $I$ satisfying
\[
I^2=I.
\]

Thus $I$ is idempotent.

More generally,
\[
I^n=I,\qquad n\geq1.
\]

The ordinary multiplication rule involving zero remains unchanged:
\[
0x=x0=0
\]
for every algebraic value $x$, including
\[
0I=I0=0.
\]

These multiplication rules are fundamental. In particular, the introduction
of $I$ does not alter the zero-preserving property of multiplication.

\subsection{The underlying algebra}

One possible algebraic carrier for the values is
\[
A=\mathbb{R}[I]/(I^2-I).
\]

Every element can be represented as
\[
a+bI,\qquad a,b\in\mathbb{R}.
\]

Addition is defined componentwise:
\[
(a+bI)+(c+dI)
=
(a+c)+(b+d)I.
\]

Multiplication follows from $I^2=I$:
\[
(a+bI)(c+dI)
=
ac+(ad+bc+bd)I.
\]

There is a useful representation
\[
a+bI\longmapsto(a,a+b)
\]
which identifies this algebra with
\[
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}.
\]

Under this representation,
\[
I\longmapsto(0,1).
\]

Consequently,
\[
I(1-I)=0.
\]

Thus $I$ is a zero divisor. This is not a defect of the construction; it is an
intentional property of the underlying algebra.

\section{Division Is a Separate Calculus}

The central principle of this proposal is:

\begin{quote}
Division is a computational operation with a restricted ordinary domain,
not an unrestricted binary operation on all algebraic values.
\end{quote}

For an ordinary denominator $b$ satisfying
\[
b\notin\{0,I\},
\]
division retains its ordinary meaning.

When the denominator is $0$ or $I$, ordinary division is not invoked.
Instead, the division expression is resolved using explicit singular rules.

This distinction is important because the notation
\[
\frac{1}{0}=I
\]
must not be interpreted as the statement that $I$ is an ordinary
multiplicative inverse of $0$.

It is a division-calculus rule.

\subsection{Resolution notation}

To emphasize the distinction between ordinary equality and division
resolution, we may write
\[
E\rightsquigarrow V
\]
to mean that the division expression $E$ resolves to the algebraic value
$V$.

Thus
\[
\frac{1}{0}\rightsquigarrow I
\]
means that the division expression $1/0$ resolves to the value $I$.

It does not mean that ordinary multiplicative inversion has been extended
to zero.

\section{Fundamental Singular Division Rules}

The fundamental rules are:

\begin{rulethm}[Division by zero]
For every algebraic numerator $a$,
\[
\frac{a}{0}\rightsquigarrow aI.
\]
\end{rule}

In particular,
\[
\frac{1}{0}\rightsquigarrow I
\]
and
\[
\frac{0}{0}\rightsquigarrow0I=0.
\]

\begin{rulethm}[Division by $I$]
For every algebraic numerator $a$,
\[
\frac{a}{I}\rightsquigarrow a0.
\]
\end{rule}

Therefore,
\[
\frac{1}{I}\rightsquigarrow0,
\]
\[
\frac{I}{I}\rightsquigarrow I0=0,
\]
and
\[
\frac{0}{I}\rightsquigarrow0.
\]

These rules can be viewed as arising from the reciprocal-resolution
identities
\[
\frac{1}{0}\rightsquigarrow I,
\qquad
\frac{1}{I}\rightsquigarrow0.
\]

They are resolution rules, not ordinary inverse identities.

\section{Zero-Preserving Multiplication}

The introduction of $I$ does not modify multiplication by zero.

For all values $x$,
\[
0x=x0=0.
\]

Hence
\[
0I=I0=0.
\]

This has an important consequence:

\[
\left(\frac{1}{0}\right)0
\rightsquigarrow I0
=0.
\]

It is therefore \emph{not} valid to apply the familiar inverse-cancellation
intuition
\[
\left(\frac{1}{0}\right)0=1.
\]

The expression $1/0$ has resolved to the value $I$; it has not established
that $I$ is a multiplicative inverse of zero.

\section{Singular Factors in Denominators}

A central rule of the calculus is that singular factors in a denominator
must be resolved before ordinary multiplication or simplification of those
factors is performed.

\subsection{A zero factor}

For an ordinary nonzero $c$,
\[
\frac{x}{0c}
\rightsquigarrow
\frac{xI}{c}.
\]

The zero factor contributes $I$ to the numerator.

Importantly, one must not first apply the ordinary algebraic identity
\[
0c=0
\]
and then replace the denominator by $0$.

Doing so would lose the factor structure that the division calculus uses.

Thus, in general,
\[
\frac{x}{0c}\neq\frac{x}{0}.
\]

The right-hand side is
\[
xI,
\]
whereas the factor-sensitive expression gives
\[
\frac{xI}{c}.
\]

\subsection{An $I$ factor}

Similarly, for ordinary nonzero $c$,
\[
\frac{x}{Ic}
\rightsquigarrow
\frac{x0}{c}
=0.
\]

Thus a denominator factor $I$ contributes a factor $0$ to the numerator.

\subsection{Multiple singular factors}

For multiple zero factors,
\[
\frac{x}{0\,0\,c}
\rightsquigarrow
\frac{xII}{c}
=
\frac{xI}{c}.
\]

The idempotence relation
\[
I^2=I
\]
then applies after the singular factors have been transferred.

Likewise,
\[
\frac{x}{I I c}
\rightsquigarrow
\frac{x00}{c}
=0.
\]

\section{Right-Associated Division}

Chained division is interpreted from the bottom upward, or equivalently,
right-associatively.

Thus
\[
a/b/c
\]
means
\[
\frac{a}{b/c},
\]
not
\[
\frac{a/b}{c}.
\]

This convention is particularly important in the presence of singular
values.

\subsection{Example: $1/0/0$}

Consider
\[
1/0/0.
\]

By the right-association rule,
\[
1/0/0
\equiv
\frac{1}{0/0}.
\]

The inner division generates the singular-factor representation
\[
0/0\rightsquigarrow0I.
\]

Because this expression occurs as the denominator of an outer division, its
singular-factor structure must be retained until the outer denominator is
resolved:

\[
\frac{1}{0/0}
=
\frac{1}{0I}.
\]

The two singular denominator factors are transferred to the numerator:

\[
\frac{1}{0I}
\rightsquigarrow
I0
=
0.
\]

Therefore,
\[
\boxed{1/0/0=0}.
\]

This must not be transformed into
\[
(1/0)/0.
\]

The latter is a different expression and is not the interpretation of the
unchained notation $1/0/0$.

\subsection{Example: $I/0$}

Separately,
\[
\frac{I}{0}
\rightsquigarrow II
=I.
\]

Thus
\[
\boxed{I/0=I}
\]
while
\[
\boxed{1/0/0=0}.
\]

There is no contradiction because these are different division expressions.

\section{Division by $I$}

The same principles apply to $I$.

For example,
\[
\frac{I}{I}
\rightsquigarrow I0
=0.
\]

Thus
\[
\boxed{I/I=0}.
\]

There is no cancellation of the two occurrences of $I$.

Likewise,
\[
\frac{1}{I}
\rightsquigarrow0.
\]

The expression $I/I$ is therefore fundamentally different from ordinary
expressions $a/a$ with an ordinary nonzero $a$.

\section{Cancellation Rules}

Ordinary cancellation is restricted to the ordinary domain.

For an ordinary nonzero $a$,
\[
\frac{a}{a}=1.
\]

However,
\[
\frac{0}{0}=0
\]
and
\[
\frac{I}{I}=0.
\]

Consequently, the rule
\[
\frac{a}{a}=1
\]
cannot be extended to $a=0$ or $a=I$.

Similarly,
\[
\left(\frac{a}{0}\right)0
=
aI0
=
0,
\]
not $a$.

The same applies to cancellation through $I$:
\[
\left(\frac{a}{I}\right)I
=
a0I
=
0.
\]

Therefore, neither $0$ nor $I$ may be cancelled in the manner of an
ordinary nonzero denominator.

\section{Why Ordinary Denominator Simplification Is Restricted}

The framework deliberately distinguishes algebraic equality from
rewriting inside an unresolved division expression.

For example, the underlying algebra satisfies
\[
0=2\cdot0.
\]

However, it does not follow that
\[
\frac{1}{0}
=
\frac{1}{2\cdot0}.
\]

The left side resolves to
\[
I,
\]
while the factor-sensitive right side resolves to
\[
\frac{I}{2}.
\]

Therefore,
\[
\frac{1}{0}\neq\frac{1}{2\cdot0}
\]
under the division calculus.

This is not a contradiction in the underlying algebra. It means that
ordinary equality of algebraic values is not an unrestricted rewrite rule
inside an unresolved singular denominator.

The denominator carries structural information relevant to division
resolution.

\section{Nested Division}

Nested division must preserve singular structure until the containing
division has been resolved.

Consider
\[
\frac{x}{y/0},
\]
where $y$ is an ordinary nonzero value.

The inner division resolves to
\[
\frac{y}{0}\rightsquigarrow yI.
\]

Therefore the outer denominator becomes
\[
yI.
\]

The singular factor $I$ is transferred to the numerator as $0$:

\[
\frac{x}{yI}
\rightsquigarrow
\frac{x0}{y}
=
0.
\]

Hence
\[
\boxed{\frac{x}{y/0}=0}
\qquad(y\neq0).
\]

The same structural principle applies when the inner division itself
contains multiple singular factors.

The important rule is that an inner division expression cannot first be
collapsed to an ordinary algebraic value if doing so would erase singular
factor information needed by an outer denominator.

\section{Context-Sensitive Evaluation}

The preceding rules imply that the calculus is not purely compositional with
respect to the ordinary algebraic value of every subexpression.

For example, as a standalone expression,
\[
\frac00\rightsquigarrow0I=0.
\]

But when the expression occurs inside an outer denominator,
\[
\frac{1}{0/0},
\]
the singular-factor representation
\[
0I
\]
must be preserved:

\[
\frac{1}{0/0}
=
\frac{1}{0I}
\rightsquigarrow I0
=0.
\]

If the inner expression were first replaced by its normalized value $0$,
one would obtain
\[
\frac10=I,
\]
which is not the prescribed result.

Therefore the calculus distinguishes between:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item the normalized algebraic value of a completed expression; and
    \item the structured representation of a division expression while it
    remains part of a denominator.
\end{enumerate}

This distinction is a fundamental part of the proposed calculus.

\section{Forbidden Transformations}

The following transformations are not generally valid when singular
denominators are involved.

\subsection{Cancellation of singular factors}

Invalid:
\[
\frac{I}{I}=1.
\]

Correct:
\[
\frac{I}{I}=0.
\]

\subsection{Cancellation through zero}

Invalid:
\[
\left(\frac{a}{0}\right)0=a.
\]

Correct:
\[
\left(\frac{a}{0}\right)0=aI0=0.
\]

\subsection{Replacing a singular denominator by an algebraically equal
expression}

Invalid:
\[
\frac{1}{0}=\frac{1}{2\cdot0}.
\]

The two denominators have different factor structures under the division
calculus.

\subsection{Reassociating chained division}

Invalid:
\[
1/0/0=(1/0)/0.
\]

The prescribed interpretation is
\[
1/0/0=1/(0/0).
\]

\subsection{Premature normalization}

Invalid:
\[
\frac{1}{0/0}
\rightarrow
\frac10
\rightarrow I.
\]

The prescribed evaluation is
\[
\frac{1}{0/0}
\rightarrow
\frac{1}{0I}
\rightarrow
I0
\rightarrow0.
\]

\subsection{Ordinary fraction cancellation}

The familiar identity
\[
\frac{a/b}{c/d}=\frac{ad}{bc}
\]
cannot be applied without checking that all relevant denominators and
intermediate division expressions lie within the ordinary division domain.

The singular calculus takes precedence over such transformations.

\section{Ordinary Arithmetic Is Preserved}

The proposal does not seek to replace ordinary arithmetic.

If all denominators encountered during evaluation are ordinary nonzero
values, the standard rules of arithmetic apply.

For example, if
\[
a,b,c\neq0
\]
and all intermediate expressions remain in the ordinary domain, then
standard identities such as
\[
\frac{a}{b}\frac{b}{c}=\frac{a}{c}
\]
remain valid.

The special rules are activated only when $0$ or $I$ enters a denominator.

This gives the calculus a natural boundary:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
\textbf{Ordinary denominator} & ordinary division rules \\
\textbf{Denominator $0$} & transfer $0\mapsto I$ to numerator \\
\textbf{Denominator $I$} & transfer $I\mapsto0$ to numerator \\
\textbf{Nested division} & preserve singular structure until outer resolution \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\section{Basic Resolution Table}

For the singular numerators and denominators, the rules give:

\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
/ & 0 & I\\
\hline
0 & 0 & 0\\
I & I & 0
\end{array}
\]

Specifically,

\[
\frac00=0,
\qquad
\frac0I=0,
\]
\[
\frac I0=I,
\qquad
\frac II=0.
\]

This table should not be interpreted as ordinary field division. It is a
summary of the singular division-resolution rules.

\section{Relationship Between the Two Layers}

The framework can therefore be viewed as having two distinct mathematical
layers.

\subsection{Value layer}

The value layer contains algebraic objects such as
\[
0,\;1,\;I,\;a+bI
\]
with ordinary addition and multiplication satisfying
\[
I^2=I,
\qquad
0x=0.
\]

\subsection{Division-expression layer}

The expression layer determines how notation involving `/` is resolved.

The division layer has its own structural rules concerning:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the ordinary domain of division;
    \item singular denominators;
    \item factor-sensitive denominator processing;
    \item right-associated chained division;
    \item preservation of singular structure in nested denominators.
\end{itemize}

The two layers should not be conflated.

In particular, the statement
\[
1/0\rightsquigarrow I
\]
belongs to the division layer, while
\[
I0=0
\]
belongs to the value layer.

There is no rule that allows the former to be interpreted as an ordinary
inverse identity.

\section{Conceptual Interpretation}

The proposal can be summarized by the following principle:

\begin{quote}
A fraction is a representation of a division operation. Division is
ordinary only when its denominator is outside the singular set
$\{0,I\}$. When a singular denominator occurs, the expression is resolved
using explicit singular-factor rules rather than ordinary inverse
arithmetic.
\end{quote}

Under this interpretation, $I$ is not ``infinity,'' nor is it an ordinary
multiplicative inverse of zero.

It is a distinguished algebraic value associated with the singular division
expression $1/0$.

The zero-preserving rule
\[
0I=0
\]
remains intact.

This makes the framework fundamentally different from approaches in which
division by zero is interpreted as an absorbing infinity-like value or in
which zero is assigned an ordinary multiplicative inverse.

\section{Examples}

\subsection{Basic examples}

\[
\frac10=I
\]

\[
\frac1I=0
\]

\[
\frac00=0
\]

\[
\frac0I=0
\]

\[
\frac I0=I
\]

\[
\frac II=0.
\]

\subsection{Products}

\[
\left(\frac10\right)0
=
I0
=
0.
\]

\[
\left(\frac10\right)I
=
I^2
=
I.
\]

\subsection{Factor-sensitive denominators}

For $c\neq0$,

\[
\frac{x}{0c}
=
\frac{xI}{c}.
\]

For $c\neq0$,

\[
\frac{x}{Ic}
=
\frac{x0}{c}
=
0.
\]

\subsection{Chained division}

\[
1/0/0
=
\frac1{0/0}
=
\frac1{0I}
=

=

\]

\subsection{Nested singular division}

For ordinary nonzero $y$,

\[
\frac{x}{y/0}
=
\frac{x}{yI}
=
\frac{x0}{y}
=
0.
\]

\section{Consistency Requirements}

The proposal should not be understood as claiming that every familiar
identity of field arithmetic remains valid.

Instead, consistency requires that the permitted rewrite system respect the
division-expression rules.

In particular, the following principles are required:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A singular denominator must be processed before ordinary
    denominator simplification.
    
    \item A singular factor generated by an inner division must be retained
    when that division expression occurs inside an outer denominator.
    
    \item Division expressions are right-associated unless explicitly
    parenthesized otherwise.
    
    \item Parentheses are semantically significant in division expressions.
    
    \item Cancellation is permitted only for ordinary nonzero denominators.
    
    \item Equality of resolved algebraic values does not automatically
    license substitution inside an unresolved division expression.
    
    \item The relations
    \[
    1/0\rightsquigarrow I,
    \qquad
    1/I\rightsquigarrow0
    \]
    are division-resolution rules and must not be interpreted as ordinary
    multiplicative inverse equations.
\end{enumerate}

These restrictions prevent the standard algebraic transformations from
producing conflicting results.

\section{Discussion}

The principal novelty of the proposed framework is not merely the
introduction of an idempotent element. Idempotent extensions of algebraic
systems are well known, as are several mathematical frameworks that assign
special meanings to division-by-zero expressions.

The distinctive feature proposed here is the combination of:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item a distinguished idempotent element $I$;
    \item preservation of ordinary zero multiplication,
    \[
    0I=0;
    \]
    \item explicit singular division resolution,
    \[
    a/0\rightsquigarrow aI,
    \qquad
    a/I\rightsquigarrow a0;
    \]
    \item factor-sensitive treatment of singular denominators;
    \item right-associated, bottom-up evaluation of chained division;
    \item preservation of singular-factor structure through nested
    denominators; and
    \item explicit rejection of ordinary cancellation and denominator
    rewriting in singular contexts.
\end{enumerate}

The resulting object is therefore better viewed as a
\emph{division calculus over an algebraic carrier} than as a conventional
field-like structure equipped with a totalized binary division operation.

\section{Open Formal Questions}

Several questions remain for a complete formal development.

\subsection{Formal grammar}

A rigorous grammar should distinguish ordinary algebraic expressions from
division expressions whose denominator remains unresolved.

\subsection{Operational semantics}

The resolution rules should be given as an ordered or context-sensitive
rewriting system so that expressions such as
\[
\frac1{0/0}
\]
have exactly the intended evaluation path.

\subsection{Confluence}

Because ordinary algebraic simplification is restricted during division
resolution, the resulting rewrite system should be studied for confluence
under its permitted rules.

\subsection{Normal forms}

A formal normal-form representation should specify when singular factors
are retained and when ordinary algebraic normalization is allowed.

\subsection{Equational theory}

The system should distinguish equations between resolved values from
rewriting rules applicable to unresolved division expressions.

These distinctions are essential for determining precisely which familiar
algebraic identities survive and which do not.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper proposes a zero-preserving division calculus built around a
distinguished idempotent element $I$.

The essential definitions are

\[
I^2=I,
\]

\[
0x=x0=0,
\]

\[
\frac{a}{0}\rightsquigarrow aI,
\]

and

\[
\frac{a}{I}\rightsquigarrow a0.
\]

The central conceptual distinction is that $I$ is an algebraic value produced
by a special division-resolution rule; it is not the ordinary multiplicative
inverse of zero.

Division is therefore not treated as an unrestricted binary operation.
Expressions containing singular denominators are evaluated according to a
specific syntax-sensitive calculus. Singular factors are transferred from
denominator to numerator, ordinary simplification is delayed until the
singular structure has been resolved, and chained division is evaluated
bottom-up/right-associatively.

Under these rules,

\[
\frac10=I,
\qquad
\frac1I=0,
\qquad
\frac00=0,
\qquad
\frac II=0,
\]

while

\[
1/0/0
=
\frac1{0/0}
=
\frac1{0I}
=
I0
=
0.
\]

The framework therefore does not attempt to preserve all identities of
ordinary field arithmetic. Instead, it defines a different computational
semantics for division while retaining the familiar zero-preserving
multiplication and a well-defined algebraic carrier.

A complete mathematical treatment requires a formal syntax, operational
semantics, normal-form theory, and a proof of consistency/confluence for the
resulting rewrite system. Those developments constitute the natural next
stage of the theory.
