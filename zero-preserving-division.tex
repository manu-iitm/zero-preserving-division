\title{\textbf{A Zero-Preserving, Singularity-Sensitive Division Calculus\\
with an Idempotent Element}}

\author{}
\date{}

\begin{abstract}
This paper proposes a division calculus in which division by zero is
represented by a distinguished idempotent element $\I$, while ordinary
multiplication by zero remains zero.  The central definitions are
\[
    \I := \frac{1}{0},
    \qquad
    0 := \frac{1}{\I},
    \qquad
    \I^2=\I,
    \qquad
    0\I=0.
\]
Division by zero is defined by
\[
    \frac{a}{0}:=a\I,
\]
and consequently
\[
    \frac{0}{0}=0.
\]

The calculus differs from ordinary totalized division systems in that
division expressions retain information about singular denominator
factors.  In particular, a zero denominator factor contributes $\I$,
whereas an $\I$ denominator factor contributes $0$.  These rules are
applied recursively when nested divisions are evaluated.

This leads to a distinction between the ordinary algebra of normalized
values and the syntax-sensitive evaluation of division expressions.
The underlying algebra is the idempotent extension
$\mathbb{R}[\I]/(\I^2-\I)$, while division is equipped with an additional
factor-resolution calculus.  The resulting system preserves ordinary
zero multiplication while providing a deterministic treatment of
division-by-zero and nested singular expressions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Division by zero is normally excluded from elementary algebra because
the inverse of zero does not exist in a field.  Several algebraic
frameworks have nevertheless been developed in which division is made
total by assigning a value to expressions such as $1/0$; examples include
wheels, meadows, and common meadows
\cite{carlstrom2004,wheels2009,noninvolutive2015,commonmeadows2015}.

The present proposal explores a different design objective.

We introduce a distinguished element $\I$ satisfying
\[
    \I := \frac{1}{0}
\]
together with the idempotence law
\[
    \I^2=\I.
\]
Unlike systems in which the introduction of a special inverse of zero
changes the behavior of multiplication by zero, this proposal retains
the ordinary rule
\[
    0x=x0=0
\]
for every algebraic element $x$, including $x=\I$.

The resulting system is therefore characterized by the combination
\[
    \frac{1}{0}=\I,
    \qquad
    \I^2=\I,
    \qquad
    0\I=0.
\]

A second defining feature is that zero and $\I$ are treated as reciprocal
singular factors:
\[
    \I=\frac{1}{0},
    \qquad
    0=\frac{1}{\I}.
\]
Consequently, singular factors must be resolved recursively rather than
simply treating every denominator expression as an ordinary algebraic
value.

The goal of this paper is to give a precise algebraic and operational
description of this calculus.

\section{Design Requirements}

The proposed system is intended to satisfy the following requirements.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{R\arabic*.}]
    \item \textbf{Division by zero is defined.}

    Every expression $a/0$ has a value.

    \item \textbf{Zero multiplication is preserved.}

    For every algebraic element $x$,
    \[
        0x=x0=0.
    \]

    \item \textbf{The singular element is idempotent.}

    \[
        \I^2=\I.
    \]

    \item \textbf{Zero over zero is zero.}

    \[
        \frac{0}{0}=0.
    \]

    \item \textbf{Ordinary division is preserved when the denominator
    is nonzero.}

    For $b\neq0$,
    \[
        \frac{a}{b}b=a.
    \]

    \item \textbf{Singular factors are preserved during division
    evaluation.}

    A denominator factor evaluating to zero contributes $\I$.
    A denominator factor evaluating to $\I$ contributes zero.

    \item \textbf{Nested singular expressions are resolved recursively.}

    Definitions involving $0$ and $\I$ remain active even when those
    elements arise as intermediate results of a calculation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Underlying Algebra}

\subsection{Definition of the algebra}

Let
\[
    A=\mathbb{R}[\I]/(\I^2-\I).
\]

Every element of $A$ can be written uniquely as
\[
    a+b\I,
    \qquad a,b\in\mathbb{R}.
\]

Addition is given by
\[
    (a+b\I)+(c+d\I)
    =(a+c)+(b+d)\I.
\]

Multiplication is determined by $\I^2=\I$:
\begin{align*}
    (a+b\I)(c+d\I)
    &=
    ac+ad\I+bc\I+bd\I^2\\
    &=
    ac+(ad+bc+bd)\I.
\end{align*}

\subsection{Concrete representation}

The algebra has the concrete representation
\[
    a+b\I
    \longmapsto
    (a,a+b)
\]
under which
\[
    \I\longmapsto(0,1).
\]

Thus
\[
    A\cong\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}.
\]

In particular,
\[
    \I(1-\I)=0.
\]

Therefore $\I$ is a nonzero zero divisor in the underlying algebra.

This is intentional.  The distinguished element $\I$ is not an ordinary
inverse of zero in a field-theoretic sense.

\section{Definitions of Zero and the Singular Element}

The two fundamental singular definitions are

\begin{definition}[Singular reciprocal pair]
The distinguished element $\I$ and zero are related by
\[
    \boxed{\I:=\frac{1}{0}},
    \qquad
    \boxed{0:=\frac{1}{\I}}.
\]
\end{definition}

These definitions are not ordinary inverse identities in the field
sense.  In particular, one must not infer
\[
    \I0=1.
\]

Instead, multiplication remains
\[
    \boxed{\I0=0}.
\]

Thus the expression $1/0$ evaluates to $\I$, while multiplying $\I$ by
zero still gives zero.

This distinction is fundamental to the calculus.

\section{Division by Zero}

Division by zero is defined by

\begin{definition}[Division by zero]
For every $a\in A$,
\[
    \boxed{\frac{a}{0}:=a\I.}
\]
\end{definition}

Consequently,
\[
    \frac{0}{0}=0\I=0.
\]

Thus division by zero does not introduce an exceptional undefined value.

For example,
\[
    \frac{1}{0}=\I,
\]
\[
    \frac{5}{0}=5\I,
\]
and
\[
    \frac{0}{0}=0.
\]

Notice that
\[
    \left(\frac{a}{0}\right)0
    =(a\I)0
    =0,
\]
rather than $a$.

Therefore the ordinary cancellation identity
\[
    \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)b=a
\]
must retain the condition $b\neq0$.

\section{Ordinary Division}

For an ordinary nonzero denominator $b$,
\[
    \frac{a}{b}
\]
retains its usual meaning.

Thus
\[
    \frac{a}{b}b=a,
    \qquad b\neq0.
\]

The system therefore agrees with ordinary field arithmetic whenever no
singular denominator is encountered.

\section{Factor-Sensitive Division}

The central operational feature of the calculus is that a denominator
is treated as a collection of factors during division evaluation.

Suppose a division expression has denominator factors
\[
    d_1d_2\cdots d_n.
\]

The factors are evaluated individually before singular normalization.

A factor may resolve to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item a nonzero ordinary element;
    \item $0$;
    \item $\I$.
\end{itemize}

The singular factors are then resolved according to

\[
    \boxed{0\text{ in a denominator }\longrightarrow \I}
\]

and

\[
    \boxed{\I\text{ in a denominator }\longrightarrow 0}.
\]

These rules are consequences of the reciprocal definitions

\[
    \I=\frac{1}{0},
    \qquad
    0=\frac{1}{\I}.
\]

They are applied recursively.

\subsection{Zero denominator factor}

For a denominator containing a zero factor,
\[
    \frac{x}{0c}
\]
with $c\neq0$, the zero factor contributes $\I$:

\[
    \frac{x}{0c}
    :=
    \frac{x\I}{c}.
\]

Thus
\[
    \boxed{\frac{x}{0c}=\frac{x\I}{c}}.
\]

Multiple zero factors do not produce higher powers after
normalization because
\[
    \I^n=\I,
    \qquad n\geq1.
\]

Therefore
\[
    \frac{x}{0\cdot0\cdot c}
    =
    \frac{x\I^2}{c}
    =
    \frac{x\I}{c}.
\]

\subsection{Singular $\I$ denominator factor}

Conversely, if an evaluated denominator contains $\I$, then the
definition
\[
    0=\frac1\I
\]
means that the $\I$ factor contributes zero.

For example, for $y\neq0$,
\[
    \frac{x}{y\I}
\]
resolves as
\[
    \frac{x}{y}\,0.
\]

Hence
\[
    \boxed{\frac{x}{y\I}=0,\qquad y\neq0.}
\]

This is the reciprocal counterpart of the rule
\[
    \frac{x}{0y}=\frac{x\I}{y}.
\]

\section{Nested Division}

Nested division is evaluated recursively.

Consider
\[
    \frac{x}{y/0}.
\]

First evaluate the inner division:
\[
    \frac{y}{0}=y\I.
\]

Therefore
\[
    \frac{x}{y/0}
    =
    \frac{x}{y\I}.
\]

If $y\neq0$, the $\I$ in the denominator resolves to zero:
\[
    \frac{x}{y\I}
    =
    \frac{x}{y}\,0
    =
    0.
\]

Therefore
\[
    \boxed{
    \frac{x}{y/0}=0,
    \qquad y\neq0.
    }
\]

If $y=0$, then
\[
    \frac{y}{0}
    =
    \frac00
    =
    0,
\]
so
\[
    \frac{x}{y/0}
    =
    \frac{x}{0}
    =
    x\I.
\]

Hence the complete rule is

\[
\boxed{
\frac{x}{y/0}
=
\begin{cases}
0, & y\neq0,\\[4pt]
x\I, & y=0.
\end{cases}}
\]

This illustrates why singular definitions must remain active throughout
evaluation.  The intermediate value $\I$ is not treated as an ordinary
nonzero denominator.

\section{Recursive Normalization}

The evaluation procedure can be stated as follows.

\begin{definition}[Recursive division normalization]
To evaluate a division expression:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Evaluate all nested subexpressions.
    \item Preserve the factor structure of the denominator.
    \item Classify each denominator factor as ordinary, zero, or $\I$.
    \item Replace every zero denominator factor by an $\I$ contribution.
    \item Replace every $\I$ denominator factor by a zero contribution.
    \item Evaluate the remaining ordinary nonzero factors using ordinary
    division.
    \item Apply the algebraic identities
    \[
        \I^2=\I,
        \qquad
        0x=0.
    \]
    \item Repeat until a normalized algebraic value is obtained.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

The important point is that steps 3--5 occur before arbitrary algebraic
collapse of a denominator expression.

\section{Grouping and Factor Structure}

Factor structure matters during singular evaluation.

For example,
\[
    \frac{x}{0c}
\]
is evaluated as
\[
    \frac{x\I}{c}.
\]

Similarly,
\[
    \frac{x}{0\cdot0\cdot c}
    =
    \frac{x\I^2}{c}
    =
    \frac{x\I}{c}.
\]

More generally,
\[
    \frac{x}{d_1d_2\cdots d_n}
\]
is evaluated by examining each $d_i$ before applying ordinary
simplifications that would erase the singular factor information.

This means that ordinary algebraic equality of denominator values is not
always sufficient to determine the semantics of a pending division
expression.

For example,
\[
    0=0\cdot2
\]
in the underlying algebra, but

\[
    \frac{1}{0}=\I
\]
whereas factor-sensitive evaluation of
\[
    \frac{1}{0\cdot2}
\]
gives
\[
    \frac{\I}{2}.
\]

Thus the division calculus distinguishes a denominator expression's
factor structure from its already-collapsed algebraic value.

\section{Equality and Evaluation Context}

The previous observation requires an explicit distinction between two
levels.

\subsection{Algebraic equality}

After normalization, ordinary algebraic equality applies.

For example,
\[
    0\cdot2=0.
\]

\subsection{Pending division expressions}

Before a division expression has been normalized, denominator factors
retain semantic information.

Consequently, the transformation
\[
    0c\longrightarrow0
\]
is not generally permitted when $0c$ occurs as an unresolved denominator
whose singular factors have not yet been processed.

This is not a claim that
\[
    0c\neq0
\]
in the underlying algebra.

Rather, it is a restriction on evaluation order for the division
operator.

The calculus is therefore best viewed as a syntax-sensitive division
calculus built over an ordinary algebraic carrier.

\section{Algebraic Properties}

\subsection{Idempotence}

By definition,
\[
    \I^2=\I.
\]

Consequently,
\[
    \I^n=\I
    \qquad(n\geq1).
\]

\subsection{Zero multiplication}

For every $x\in A$,
\[
    0x=x0=0.
\]

In particular,
\[
    0\I=\I0=0.
\]

\subsection{Addition of singular divisions}

Because
\[
    \frac{a}{0}=a\I,
\]
we obtain
\[
    \frac{a}{0}+\frac{b}{0}
    =
    a\I+b\I
    =
    (a+b)\I
    =
    \frac{a+b}{0}.
\]

Thus
\[
    \boxed{
    \frac{a}{0}+\frac{b}{0}
    =
    \frac{a+b}{0}.
    }
\]

\subsection{Multiplication of singular divisions}

Similarly,
\begin{align*}
    \frac{a}{0}\frac{b}{0}
    &=
    (a\I)(b\I)\\
    &=
    ab\I^2\\
    &=
    ab\I\\
    &=
    \frac{ab}{0}.
\end{align*}

Hence
\[
    \boxed{
    \frac{a}{0}\frac{b}{0}
    =
    \frac{ab}{0}.
    }
\]

\section{Valid and Restricted Transformations}

The following transformations are valid.

\begin{align*}
    \I^2 &= \I,\\
    \I^n &= \I,\qquad n\geq1,\\
    0x &= 0,\\
    x0 &= 0,\\
    \frac{a}{0} &= a\I,\\
    \frac00 &= 0,\\
    \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)b &= a,\qquad b\neq0,\\
    \left(\frac{a}{0}\right)0 &= 0,\\
    \frac{a}{0}+\frac{b}{0}
        &=\frac{a+b}{0},\\
    \frac{a}{0}\frac{b}{0}
        &=\frac{ab}{0},\\
    \frac{x}{0c}
        &=\frac{x\I}{c},\qquad c\neq0,\\
    \frac{x}{0\cdot0\cdot c}
        &=\frac{x\I}{c},\qquad c\neq0,\\
    \frac{x}{y\I}
        &=0,\qquad y\neq0.
\end{align*}

The following transformations are not generally valid.

\begin{align*}
    \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)b &=a
        &&\text{without }b\neq0,\\
    \left(\frac{a}{0}\right)0 &=a,\\
    \frac{x}{0c}&=\frac{x}{0},\\
    \frac{x}{y\I}&=\frac{x}{y}\I,\\
    0c&\longrightarrow0
        &&\text{before singular denominator resolution}.
\end{align*}

In particular, cancellation through zero or $\I$ is not permitted.

\section{Examples}

\begin{example}
Consider
\[
    \frac{5}{0}.
\]

By definition,
\[
    \frac{5}{0}=5\I.
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Consider
\[
    \frac{0}{0}.
\]

Then
\[
    \frac00=0\I=0.
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Consider
\[
    \frac{x}{0c},
    \qquad c\neq0.
\]

The zero denominator factor contributes $\I$:
\[
    \frac{x}{0c}
    =
    \frac{x\I}{c}.
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Consider
\[
    \frac{x}{0\cdot0\cdot c},
    \qquad c\neq0.
\]

Both zero factors contribute $\I$:
\[
    \frac{x}{0\cdot0\cdot c}
    =
    \frac{x\I^2}{c}
    =
    \frac{x\I}{c}.
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Consider
\[
    \frac{x}{y/0},
    \qquad y\neq0.
\]

First,
\[
    \frac{y}{0}=y\I.
\]

Therefore
\[
    \frac{x}{y/0}
    =
    \frac{x}{y\I}.
\]

The $\I$ denominator factor contributes zero:
\[
    \frac{x}{y\I}
    =
    \frac{x}{y}0
    =
    0.
\]

Hence
\[
    \boxed{\frac{x}{y/0}=0,\qquad y\neq0.}
\]
\end{example}

\begin{example}
Consider
\[
    \frac{x}{0/0}.
\]

Since
\[
    \frac00=0,
\]
we obtain
\[
    \frac{x}{0/0}
    =
    \frac{x}{0}
    =
    x\I.
\]
\end{example}

\section{Relation to Existing Frameworks}

The proposal is related conceptually to existing attempts to make
division total.

Carlström's theory of wheels provides a systematic algebraic treatment
of division by zero \cite{carlstrom2004}. Meadows provide an equational
framework in which division is totalized \cite{wheels2009}, with further
variants including non-involutive and common meadows
\cite{noninvolutive2015,commonmeadows2015}.

The present proposal differs in its design objectives.

First, ordinary multiplication by zero remains unchanged:
\[
    0x=0.
\]

Second, the singular element $\I$ is idempotent:
\[
    \I^2=\I.
\]

Third, division by zero is represented as
\[
    \frac{a}{0}=a\I,
\]
giving
\[
    \frac00=0.
\]

Fourth, the proposal explicitly treats zero and $\I$ as reciprocal
singular denominator factors:
\[
    \I=\frac10,
    \qquad
    0=\frac1\I.
\]

Finally, division expressions are evaluated using a factor-sensitive,
recursive normalization procedure rather than treating division solely
as an ordinary binary operation on already-normalized algebraic values.

These differences should be studied carefully against existing
totalized-division frameworks rather than interpreted as a claim that
the underlying idempotent extension itself is new.

\section{Novelty and Scope}

The algebra
\[
    \mathbb{R}[\I]/(\I^2-\I)
\]
is a standard type of idempotent algebraic extension, and idempotent
elements have been used in other approaches to division by zero.
Accordingly, the proposal does not claim novelty merely from introducing
an idempotent element satisfying $\I^2=\I$.

The potentially distinctive aspect is the combination of:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item preservation of ordinary zero multiplication;
    \item the definition
    \[
        \frac{1}{0}=\I;
    \]
    \item the reciprocal definition
    \[
        \frac{1}{\I}=0;
    \]
    \item the rule
    \[
        \frac{a}{0}=a\I;
    \]
    \item the resulting identity
    \[
        \frac00=0;
    \]
    \item factor-sensitive treatment of singular denominator factors;
    \item recursive resolution of both zero and $\I$ denominator factors;
    \item explicit separation between normalized algebraic equality and
    the syntax-sensitive evaluation of pending division expressions.
\end{enumerate}

A complete novelty claim would require a broader literature review,
especially of algebraic systems that combine idempotent singular
elements with totalized division and nonstandard evaluation semantics.

\section{Open Questions}

The basic division rules above are intended to be deterministic.
Several mathematical questions remain open.

\subsection{Formal rewrite-system properties}

A formal rewrite system should be developed and studied for:

\begin{itemize}
    \item termination;
    \item confluence;
    \item uniqueness of normal forms;
    \item compatibility between algebraic normalization and singular
    denominator resolution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Generalization beyond real coefficients}

The construction can be considered over other base rings or fields:
\[
    R[\I]/(\I^2-\I).
\]

It remains to characterize which properties depend on the choice of
$R$.

\subsection{Compatibility with symbolic algebra}

A computer algebra implementation would need to preserve denominator
factor structure until singular factors have been resolved.  Standard
symbolic simplification rules may therefore need to distinguish between
ordinary algebraic expressions and pending division expressions.

\subsection{Comparison with existing totalized division systems}

A complete formal comparison with wheels, meadows, common meadows, and
other division-by-zero systems is required to establish precisely which
equational and computational properties are shared and which differ.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper proposes a zero-preserving division calculus based on a
distinguished idempotent element $\I$ satisfying

\[
    \I=\frac10,
    \qquad
    \I^2=\I,
    \qquad
    0=\frac1\I,
    \qquad
    0\I=0.
\]

Division by zero is defined by

\[
    \frac{a}{0}=a\I,
\]

which implies

\[
    \frac00=0.
\]

The key semantic principle is that singular denominator factors are
resolved recursively:

\[
    0\longrightarrow\I,
    \qquad
    \I\longrightarrow0.
\]

Thus zero and $\I$ form a reciprocal pair of singular factors while
remaining distinct algebraic elements.

The underlying carrier is the idempotent algebra

\[
    \mathbb{R}[\I]/(\I^2-\I),
\]

but division requires an additional syntax-sensitive evaluation layer.
This layer preserves denominator factor information long enough to
resolve singular factors before ordinary algebraic normalization.

The resulting framework provides a precise setting in which division by
zero is defined without changing the ordinary rule that multiplication
by zero produces zero.  Its mathematical value ultimately depends on
the development of a complete formal rewrite system and a rigorous
comparison with existing totalized-division theories.
